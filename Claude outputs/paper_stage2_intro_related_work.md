# Evidence-Aware Pre-Payment Risk Assessment for UPI Transactions Using Payer Behaviour, Payee History, and an Incremental Payment Graph

# Abstract

Instant retail payment systems such as India's Unified Payments Interface (UPI) settle transfers within seconds, leaving little opportunity to intervene once a payment has been sent. An important class of losses arises from payer-authorised deception, in which the legitimate account holder is persuaded to initiate and authenticate a payment to a fraudster; because the payer behaves normally, classifiers that model only payer history can receive little signal. This study investigates a pre-payment risk-assessment architecture that evaluates a payment request before submission by combining payer behaviour with observed payee history, an incrementally maintained payer–payee graph, payment-address and QR-payload analysis, amount context, message and link indicators, and a calibrated gradient-boosting fraud probability. An aggregation rule keeps risk separate from evidence availability, treats absent evidence as unknown rather than benign or suspicious, and credits corroboration only across distinct underlying observations. Because labelled UPI fraud data are not publicly available, evaluation uses a synthetic corpus of 120,000 payments from 2,000 payers with a 1.18% fraud rate spanning social engineering, mule collection, account takeover and card testing. In an offline evaluation on a held-out test set, the expanded-feature model (V2) reaches a PR-AUC of 0.733 and a recall of 0.780 at a validation-selected threshold with a realised false-positive rate of 0.99%, compared with a PR-AUC of 0.486 for the 12-feature model (V1) retained by the working prototype. Social engineering remains the hardest scenario (recall 0.659). Because the additional V2 signals are produced by the same simulator, the results characterise the architecture under controlled conditions, not real-world performance.

# Keywords

Unified Payments Interface (UPI); pre-payment risk assessment; fraud detection; authorised push payment fraud; behavioural analytics; transaction graph; evidence aggregation; explainable machine learning

# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Background and Motivation

Retail payments in India have shifted rapidly toward the Unified Payments Interface, an interoperable real-time system in which a payer addresses a recipient through a virtual payment address (VPA), a mobile number or a QR code, and funds move between bank accounts within seconds [1]. The properties that make such systems attractive to users — immediacy, low friction and availability at any hour — also change the economics of fraud. When a transfer settles almost instantly and is difficult to reverse, the practical opportunity to prevent loss is concentrated in the short interval between the moment a payer decides to pay and the moment the payment is submitted. Detection that operates after settlement can support investigation and recovery, but it cannot restore the funds of a victim whose money has already been moved onward.

Fraud detection in payment systems has long been approached as a statistical classification problem over transaction records, in which each transaction is compared with the historical behaviour of the account that made it [2]. This framing has been productive for card fraud, where a stolen credential typically produces spending that differs from the owner's habits in amount, location, merchant or timing. Operational studies have also shown that fraud detection departs from textbook classification in important ways: fraudulent cases are rare, labels arrive with delay, fraud patterns drift, and the number of alerts that investigators can review is limited [3]. These characteristics already make the problem difficult. Instant payments add a further constraint: the relevant decision is not whether a completed transaction was fraudulent, but whether a payment that has not yet been made should proceed, proceed with a warning, or be verified first.

## 1.2 Problem Context

It is useful to distinguish two broad forms of payment fraud. In the first, an attacker gains control of an account or credential and initiates payments that the owner did not authorise. In the second, the account owner initiates and authenticates the payment personally, but does so because they have been deceived — for example by a caller impersonating a bank or government agency, a fraudulent seller, a fake refund process, or a message that manufactures urgency. The second form is commonly described as authorised push payment (APP) fraud, and it has attracted regulatory attention precisely because the victim's own authentication makes the transaction appear legitimate to the payment system [4].

This distinction matters for how detection is designed. Behavioural anomaly detection assumes that fraud leaves a trace in the payer's own activity. Consider a user who habitually makes payments in the early evening, from the same device, using the usual authentication method. If a fraudster persuades that user to transfer money at that familiar hour, from that device, for an amount within their normal range, the payer-side features may be indistinguishable from an ordinary payment. The evidence that something is wrong lies elsewhere: in who is being paid, in how that recipient has been paid by others, in the address or QR code used to identify the recipient, in the message that prompted the payment, and in any links that accompanied it. A system that reasons only about the payer is therefore structurally limited for this class of fraud, however well its classifier is trained.

Payee-side and relational information address part of this limitation. A recipient account used to collect proceeds from many victims tends to exhibit a recognisable pattern: it is recently observed, it receives payments from many unrelated payers who rarely return, and those payers may share other counterparties. Graph-based fraud detection exploits exactly this kind of relational structure, which is not visible when transactions are treated as independent records [5], [6]. Recent work has further emphasised that transaction graphs evolve continuously, so that relational evidence must be maintained incrementally rather than recomputed from scratch [5], and that temporal ordering carries information about how fraudulent behaviour develops [7].

Contextual evidence provides a further, partly independent view. In a UPI payment, the payer frequently encounters the recipient through a scanned QR payload or a typed address, and the payment is often preceded by a message, call or link. Irregularities in the payment address, a mismatch between a QR code's displayed name and the address it pays, coercive wording, or a link that imitates a known brand are observations about the payment context rather than about the payer's history. None of these signals is conclusive on its own; each is a partial and fallible observation. The value of combining them lies in the fact that they observe different aspects of the same payment request.

Combining heterogeneous evidence introduces two further difficulties. The first concerns missing information. A payee who has never been observed is not the same as a payee known to be suspicious, yet systems that replace unavailable measurements with default values can silently convert "we cannot tell" into a specific, and potentially misleading, input. This is especially consequential for new payees, which include both legitimate new merchants and newly created collection accounts. The second concerns correlated evidence. When several components of a system examine the same underlying observation — for example, two modules that both read the transaction timestamp — rewarding their agreement as if they were independent confirmations overstates the strength of the evidence. A risk assessment intended to support a human decision must also be explainable: a payer who is advised to pause needs to understand which observations motivated the advice.

## 1.3 Research Gap

Existing research offers strong components but leaves their combination for the pre-payment decision comparatively underexplored. Much of the transaction-level fraud-detection literature evaluates classifiers on completed transaction records and emphasises deviations in the payer's behaviour [2], [3]; UPI-specific studies in this vein have applied gradient-boosted ensembles with resampling to transaction features [8]. Graph-based approaches exploit relational structure effectively, but they are generally formulated as detectors over the whole graph — identifying dense collusive groups [5], [6] or learning node representations with graph neural networks [7], [9], [10] — rather than as a lightweight source of evidence for a single payment request that has not yet been made. Contextual signals such as payment-address integrity, QR payload consistency, message coercion and link reputation are seldom integrated with payer and payee behavioural evidence within one assessment. In addition, the reviewed approaches do not generally describe how unavailable evidence is represented, or how correlated signals are prevented from being counted as independent corroboration. Finally, evaluation practice remains uneven: under the class imbalance characteristic of fraud, accuracy and even ROC-AUC can present an optimistic view of performance, whereas precision–recall analysis and recall at a fixed false-positive budget better reflect operational usefulness [11], [12].

## 1.4 Proposed Approach

This study develops and evaluates an evidence-aware pre-payment risk-assessment architecture for UPI-style payments. The system is designed as an advisory decision-support layer that assesses a payment request — identified by a scanned QR payload, a VPA or a mobile number — before it is submitted. It is not a payment processor, a bank, a payment service provider or part of the national switching infrastructure, and it does not execute, settle or autonomously block payments.

For each request, the architecture evaluates a set of evidence families in parallel. Payer-side families compare the proposed payment with the payer's own history, including amount, time of day and payment velocity, and estimate a fraud probability with a calibrated gradient-boosting classifier. Payee-side families draw on observed payment history for the recipient and on an incrementally maintained payer–payee graph that records how many distinct payers have paid an address, whether they return, how payments arrived over time and how the address is connected to others. Context families examine the payment address and QR payload, the amount relative to rail limits and plausible transfers to strangers, the message that prompted the payment, and any accompanying links. Each family reports a score, whether it was able to observe anything at all, and the specific underlying facts on which its score rests. An aggregation rule then combines the available scores so that the strongest signal leads, additional signals contribute with diminishing weight, and a corroboration bonus is granted only for agreement across distinct facts. The output consists of a risk score, a measure of how much evidence was available, one of four advisory verdicts, and human-readable findings. A low-risk verdict indicates that the calculated risk lies below the configured intervention thresholds; it does not certify that a payment is safe.

Because labelled UPI fraud data held by banks, payment providers and the national switch are not publicly available, the classifier is trained and evaluated on synthetic data generated to reproduce four fraud scenarios under severe class imbalance, with deliberately overlapping legitimate behaviour. Two model generations are reported. The working prototype retains an earlier 12-feature scoring path (V1). An expanded feature representation (V2), which adds relational, graph, message, link and stated-purpose features, was trained and then frozen, and it is evaluated offline on the same held-out test set to quantify the effect of the expanded representation. The V2 results reported in this paper are therefore offline research results and not measurements of the running prototype.

## 1.5 Contributions

The principal contributions of this work are as follows.

First, the study presents an integrated pre-payment risk-assessment architecture for UPI-style payments that combines payer behavioural evidence with payee history, payer–payee relational information and contextual evidence drawn from the payment address, QR payload, amount, message and links.

Second, it proposes an evidence-aware aggregation mechanism in which each evidence family declares both its availability and the underlying observations its score relies on. This allows risk to be reported separately from evidence availability, prevents unavailable evidence from acting as either reassurance or suspicion, and limits double counting when several families rest on the same observation.

Third, it describes an incrementally maintained payer–payee graph that supplies explicit relational features — distinct payers, repeat behaviour, arrival timing, component membership and bounded neighbourhood overlap — to the assessment path without rebuilding the graph for each request, and using only information available before the payment being assessed.

Fourth, it provides a reproducible evaluation of the approach on severely imbalanced synthetic UPI-style data, in which the operating threshold is selected on a validation split and measured once on a held-out test set, and in which results are reported through precision–recall analysis, recall at a fixed false-positive budget, probability calibration, per-scenario recall and feature-group ablation.

# 2. Related Work

## 2.1 Machine-Learning-Based Payment Fraud Detection

Early statistical treatments of fraud detection established the problem's defining characteristics: fraudulent cases form a small minority, the cost of a missed fraud differs sharply from the cost of a false alarm, and fraudsters adapt to whatever detection is deployed [2]. Supervised learning over labelled transactions became the dominant paradigm as institutions accumulated histories of confirmed fraud, and ensembles of decision trees have been particularly widely used because they handle heterogeneous tabular features and non-linear interactions with little preprocessing. Histogram-based gradient boosting, which discretises continuous features to accelerate tree construction [13], is one widely used member of this family and is available in standard machine-learning libraries [14].

A recurring methodological question concerns class imbalance. One common response is to rebalance the training data, for example by synthesising additional minority examples through interpolation between neighbouring fraud cases [15]. Rebalancing can make a classifier more sensitive to the minority class, but it also changes the class prior the model learns, so the resulting scores no longer correspond directly to fraud probabilities at the true base rate. An alternative is to train on data that preserve the natural imbalance and to address the minority class through the choice of operating threshold and through probability calibration. Work grounded in operational card-fraud data has argued that realistic evaluation must take into account the limited number of alerts investigators can process, the delay with which labels become available, and concept drift [3]. That perspective shifts attention from global accuracy toward the precision of the alerts actually raised.

The present study follows the second approach. Its classifier is trained on data with the natural fraud rate, calibrated, and evaluated at a threshold chosen to respect a fixed false-positive budget. It differs from much of the machine-learning literature in that the classifier is not the whole system but one evidence family among several, whose output is combined with evidence the classifier does not observe.

## 2.2 Behavioural and Transaction-Level Fraud Detection

Behavioural fraud detection characterises what is normal for a given account — typical amounts, hours of activity, payment frequency and preferred counterparties — and scores new transactions by their deviation from that profile [2]. Velocity measures, such as the number of payments in a recent window or the time since the previous payment, capture bursts of activity typical of card testing and of an attacker draining a compromised account. Amount ratios relative to an account's own history capture unusually large transfers. These features are effective precisely when fraud alters the account's observable behaviour, as in account takeover and credential theft.

Transaction-level models share an implicit assumption: that the transaction record, together with the payer's history, contains the information needed to recognise fraud. Studies of operational card-fraud detection illustrate both the strength of this assumption and its boundaries, since they model fraud as an anomaly in a cardholder's transaction stream [3]. Authorised payment fraud weakens the assumption. When a deceived payer initiates a payment of plausible size at a familiar time, the features that characterise the payer's behaviour may carry little evidence, and the most informative observations concern the recipient and the circumstances surrounding the request.

The present work retains behavioural features — amount relative to the payer's median, deviation from the payer's usual hour, payment velocity over the previous hour and day, and whether the payer has paid this recipient before — and computes them strictly from payments that precede the one being assessed. It departs from payer-only modelling by treating these features as one view of the payment, alongside views of the payee and the context that do not depend on the payer behaving unusually.

## 2.3 Graph-Based Fraud Detection

Graph-based methods represent payers, recipients, merchants or accounts as nodes and their interactions as edges, making relational patterns explicit. A prominent line of work detects fraud as unusually dense subgraphs: groups of accounts that interact far more among themselves than chance would predict, as occurs with fake reviews, purchased followers or collusive transactions. FRAUDAR formulated such detection so that it remains effective when fraudsters add camouflage edges to legitimate nodes [6]. Dense-subgraph formulations are attractive for detecting coordinated groups because their signal arises from structure rather than from any single transaction.

Graph neural networks (GNNs) take a different route, learning node representations by aggregating information from neighbouring nodes. Their application to fraud has had to confront the observation that fraudsters can connect themselves to benign nodes so that naive neighbourhood aggregation dilutes their signal; CARE-GNN addressed this by learning which neighbours to aggregate over [9]. In financial forensics, graph convolutional networks have been applied to transaction graphs of cryptocurrency flows to classify illicit transactions [10].

These approaches demonstrate that relational structure carries information that independent transaction features do not. They are generally formulated as detectors over a graph as a whole, identifying groups or classifying nodes, and learned representations can be difficult to explain to an individual user. The present study uses relational information differently. It does not perform dense-subgraph mining and does not train a graph neural network. Instead, it maintains a payer–payee graph from which it reads a small number of explicit, interpretable quantities — for example, how many distinct payers an address has received from, how many of them have returned, and whether the address shares an unusual number of payers with other addresses — and presents these to the risk assessment as one evidence family among several.

## 2.4 Dynamic and Temporal Transaction Graphs

Payment graphs change continuously as new transactions arrive, and methods designed for static graphs must either be rerun periodically or adapted to handle updates. Spade+ addresses this problem for dense-subgraph fraud detection in an industrial setting, incrementally maintaining the peeling order on which dense-subgraph algorithms rely as edges are inserted and deleted, so that suspicious communities can be updated without recomputing them from scratch [5]. The framework also supports removing outdated transactions, which prevents long-standing legitimate communities from gradually becoming denser than fraudulent ones, and it exposes interfaces through which developers can define their own suspiciousness measures [5]. Its evaluation on industrial and public graphs reports large reductions in update time relative to static recomputation [5].

A complementary line of work treats time as information to be learned rather than a maintenance burden. PGLTSE models transactions as a continuous-time dynamic graph, keeping a recurrent memory of each node's history, and combines a local view of entity interactions with a global view of transaction relations over which risk is propagated to detect organised, gang-like fraud [7]. Evaluated on healthcare-claims, e-commerce and credit-card datasets, it is reported to outperform static and dynamic graph baselines on AUC, AUPRC, recall and F1 [7].

Both works motivate design choices in the present study, but neither is reproduced by it. Following the motivation of incremental maintenance [5], the payer–payee graph used here is updated as each payment is observed, within the same storage transaction as the corresponding payment record, so that the assessment path reads maintained quantities rather than rebuilding the graph. Following the emphasis on temporal information [7], graph and relationship features are computed only from observations that precede the payment being assessed, and they include timing quantities such as how recently and how tightly clustered a payee's payments are. The two approaches differ from the present study in purpose and method: Spade+ performs incremental dense-subgraph detection and PGLTSE learns temporal graph representations with neural networks, whereas this study derives explicit graph features for a classical calibrated classifier and a rule-based evidence aggregator.

## 2.5 UPI and Instant-Payment Fraud

Research on UPI has examined its role as a digital payment innovation and its contribution to financial inclusion and economic development [1]. Work specifically on fraud detection for UPI transactions is comparatively limited in the peer-reviewed literature. Rani et al. describe a UPI fraud detection system that applies synthetic minority oversampling, dimensionality reduction by principal component analysis, and an XGBoost classifier, integrated into a monitoring interface, and compare the classifier with several alternatives using accuracy [8]. The study illustrates that UPI-specific machine-learning pipelines are being developed. Its reported evaluation differs from the present work in two respects: performance is summarised by accuracy, which under a low fraud rate can remain high even for a classifier that misses most fraud, and the source and size of its dataset are not reported. Its accuracy figures therefore cannot be compared numerically with the precision–recall measures used here.

More broadly, the availability of labelled data constrains research in this domain. Confirmed fraud labels for UPI transactions are held by banks, payment service providers and the national switch, and are not released publicly. Synthetic simulation is an established response to this constraint in payment-fraud research: PaySim, for example, generates mobile-money transactions from an agent-based model calibrated on aggregate statistics of a real service, so that detection methods can be developed and compared without exposing customer data [16]. Synthetic data carry an evident cost in external validity, which the present study acknowledges as a principal limitation, but they allow controlled construction of fraud scenarios, preservation of realistic class imbalance and fully reproducible experiments.

## 2.6 Authorised Push Payment and Social-Engineering Fraud

Authorised push payment fraud has been examined primarily from legal and regulatory perspectives, with attention to how victims are deceived into authorising transfers and to how losses should be allocated between customers and payment providers [4]. The defining feature of this fraud type, for detection purposes, is that the payment is authenticated by its legitimate owner. Controls that verify that the right person is making the payment offer no protection when the right person has been manipulated, and the fraud may be evident only from the recipient, from the stated purpose of the payment, or from the communication that preceded it.

Graph-learning work has also noted that fraudsters increasingly combine social engineering with organised, multi-account operations [7], which suggests that recipient-side and relational evidence may be informative for this class of fraud. At the same time, message and link content are observable only when the payer supplies them, and even suspicious wording can occur in legitimate communication. The present study therefore treats message coercion and link reputation as individual evidence families with modest weight, which can corroborate payee-side and behavioural evidence but are not treated as a detector for social engineering on their own. The study's evaluation reports recall separately for the social-engineering scenario so that performance on this class is not hidden inside an aggregate figure.

## 2.7 Explainability, Calibration, and Evaluation Under Class Imbalance

The choice of evaluation measure has particular consequences when positives are rare. Receiver operating characteristic curves summarise the trade-off between true- and false-positive rates, but when negatives vastly outnumber positives, a small false-positive rate can still correspond to many false alarms per true detection. Davis and Goadrich established a one-to-one correspondence between ROC and precision–recall curves, showing that one curve dominates another in ROC space if and only if it does so in precision–recall space, while also showing that optimising the area under the ROC curve does not guarantee optimising the area under the precision–recall curve [12]. Saito and Rehmsmeier showed empirically that precision–recall plots give a more informative account of classifier performance than ROC plots on imbalanced data [11]. For fraud detection, where each alert consumes investigator attention or interrupts a customer, recall at a fixed false-positive budget and the precision of the resulting alerts are directly interpretable operating characteristics [3].

A related concern is whether predicted scores can be read as probabilities. Boosted tree ensembles tend to produce distorted probability estimates, and post-hoc calibration using sigmoid or isotonic mappings can correct this [17], [18]. Calibration matters when a probability is combined with other evidence or compared against fixed thresholds, as it is in the present architecture. The Brier score, the mean squared difference between predicted probabilities and outcomes, provides a single measure that reflects both calibration and discrimination [19].

Explainability serves different audiences in fraud detection. Model-level explanations, such as feature importance, help developers understand what a classifier has learned, and additive attribution methods such as SHAP decompose an individual prediction into contributions from its features [20]. For an advisory system addressed to a payer, however, an explanation should refer to observations the payer can understand and check, such as the age of a payee's address or a mismatch between a QR code's displayed name and its address. The present study produces findings at the level of evidence families, each tied to the observations that produced it, and complements these with model-level attributions for the classifier.

## 2.8 Research Gap

Taken together, the reviewed literature supplies each of the ingredients that a pre-payment assessment requires but seldom assembles them for that purpose. Transaction-level and behavioural methods model the payer well but are structurally weak when the payer has been persuaded to act normally [2], [3], [4]. Graph-based and temporal methods capture relational structure and its evolution [5], [6], [7], [9], [10], but they are generally formulated as whole-graph detectors or representation learners rather than as a source of explicit, per-request evidence that can be computed before a payment is made and explained to the person making it. Existing UPI-specific work has concentrated on transaction-feature classifiers evaluated by accuracy [8]. Across these strands, contextual evidence from the payment address, QR payload, message and links is rarely combined with payer and payee evidence in a single assessment, and the reviewed approaches do not generally describe how unavailable evidence is kept separate from benign evidence or how correlated signals are prevented from being counted as independent corroboration.

This leaves an opportunity to investigate whether a pre-payment architecture that combines complementary evidence families, maintains relational features incrementally, represents missing evidence explicitly and aggregates evidence with attention to the observations each family relies on can provide useful, explainable risk assessments under the severe class imbalance of payment fraud. The following section formalises this problem and states the design objectives that guide the architecture.
