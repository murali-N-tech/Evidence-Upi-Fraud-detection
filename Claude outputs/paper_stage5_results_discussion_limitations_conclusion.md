# 10. Results

## 10.1 Dataset and Evaluation Summary

All predictive results are obtained on the synthetic corpus described in Section 6: 120,000 payment rows from 2,000 simulated payers, of which 1,419 (1.183%) are fraudulent, generated with seed 42. The stratified split yields 72,000 training, 18,000 validation and 30,000 held-out test rows. The test split contains 355 fraudulent payments — 182 social engineering, 80 mule collection, 73 account takeover and 20 card testing — and 29,645 legitimate payments. V1 and V2 are evaluated on the identical test rows. Every threshold was selected on the validation split under a 1% false-positive budget and applied unchanged to the test split.

The results in this section fall into two categories that should not be conflated. Sections 10.2–10.6 report the predictive performance of the classifier component under the synthetic evaluation protocol. They do not measure the complete ten-family evidence-aware verdict system, which was not benchmarked end to end, and the V2 graph features they use are generated by the simulator rather than computed by the running incremental graph. Section 10.7 reports software validation of the serving and graph components, which is a separate kind of evidence.

## 10.3 V2 Predictive Results

The frozen V2 model reaches a PR-AUC of 0.7326 and a ROC-AUC of 0.9900 on the held-out test split under the synthetic evaluation protocol (Table 5). At its validation-selected threshold of 0.1123, it raises 572 alerts: TP = 277, FP = 295, FN = 78 and TN = 29,350. This corresponds to recall 0.7803, precision 0.4843, F1 0.5976 and a realised false-positive rate of 0.00995, within the 1% budget. The Brier score is 0.00555, against 0.01169 for a constant predictor at the base rate.

The test figures are close to those obtained on validation when the threshold was chosen (validation PR-AUC 0.6995, recall 0.746 at a realised false-positive rate of 0.0100). Test performance is therefore not markedly better than the performance on the split used for the decision, which is what one would expect if the threshold had been fitted to the test data; it is consistent with the protocol having been followed. This is one split and one seed, however, and no confidence intervals or significance tests were computed, so small differences should not be over-read.

Scenario recall for V2 is 65.93% for social engineering (120 of 182), 85.00% for mule collection (68 of 80), 94.52% for account takeover (69 of 73) and 100% for card testing (20 of 20). Compared with V1 on the same rows, the gains are concentrated in the two scenarios in which the payer's own behaviour is least informative. Account-takeover and card-testing recall are unchanged. The number of legitimate payments flagged increases from 280 to 295.

These figures describe the V2 classifier on simulated data. The V2 channels that distinguish it from V1 — relationship, graph, message, link and stated intent — are produced by the same simulator that assigns the labels, and the V2 graph features are sampled rather than computed by the incremental graph. The results show what the expanded representation can extract from the simulator's signals; they are not an estimate of performance on real UPI payments.

## 10.4 V2 Ablation Results

Each configuration was re-fitted on the training split with the V1-configured gradient-boosting learner **without isotonic calibration**, given its own threshold from the validation split, and measured once on the test split. Because the models are uncalibrated and separately trained, their figures differ from those of the calibrated frozen models even for the same feature set. In particular, the full-V2 row of the ablation reaches a PR-AUC of 0.7234, whereas the frozen, calibrated V2 model reaches 0.7326 (Table 5); 0.7234 is not the V2 result, and 0.7326 is not an ablation result. The same applies to the V1 reference row, whose uncalibrated PR-AUC of 0.4996 differs from the served V1 model's 0.4860. The ablation is intended to be read for differences between its own rows.

## 10.5 Missing-Evidence Results

The observation mask hides payee history on 29,376 of the 120,000 rows (24.48%). The fraud prevalence is 1.729% among rows with hidden history and 1.005% among rows with visible history, a difference of 0.72 percentage points. The two subsets therefore have different simulated class distributions. This follows from the mask's construction — visibility rises with a payee's number of distinct payers, and simulated collection accounts tend to have fewer observed payers — and it does not show that missing history causes fraud or that it is, by itself, evidence of fraud. The automated tests of Section 9.7 check that the difference stays small and positive and that the availability indicator alone does not separate the classes strongly. At the prevalence observed among hidden rows, 98.3% of payments to unobserved payees are legitimate, which is why the architecture does not treat absence as a risk factor. The evaluation artefacts do not break down test performance by availability, so no per-subset detection figures are reported.

## 10.6 Calibration and Explainability Results

Both calibrated models improve on the constant base-rate predictor in Brier score: the Brier skill score is 0.317 for V1 and 0.525 for V2. Reliability curves were requested with ten quantile bins, but because most predictions lie near zero only four quantile bins were populated for each model, so uniform-width bins are also reported. For V2, the lowest bin (predicted probability below 0.1) contains 29,383 test payments with a mean prediction of 0.0033 and an observed fraud rate of 0.0025; in the intermediate bins, observed rates are close to or somewhat above the mean predictions (for example, a mean prediction of 0.347 against an observed rate of 0.435 in the 0.3–0.4 bin, which contains 46 payments); and the top bin contains 71 payments, all fraudulent, with a mean prediction of 0.973. The intermediate bins contain between 33 and 231 payments each, so their observed rates are estimated imprecisely. These results indicate that the isotonic calibration produces probabilities of roughly the right magnitude on this simulator; they say nothing about calibration on real data, where the base rate and score distribution may differ.

Permutation importance, computed on the calibrated models as the mean decrease in test average precision over five permutations, ranks for V1 *amount_over_user_avg* (0.283), *payee_is_new* (0.230), *txns_last_hour* (0.218), *payee_age_days* (0.193) and *payee_repeat_ratio* (0.154) highest. For V2, the highest-ranked features are *payee_is_new* (0.366), *arrival_span_days* (0.246), *amount_over_user_avg* (0.206), *msg_coercion_probability* (0.138) and *component_size* (0.121). Two of the five highest-ranked V2 features are simulated graph features and one is a simulated message feature, which is consistent with the ablation.

SHAP values were computed with a tree explainer on 200 test rows, using the uncalibrated booster of the first calibration fold. They therefore explain that booster's raw output, not the calibrated probability, and their magnitudes are on the booster's scale. By mean absolute SHAP value, *payee_is_new* is the largest contributor in both V1 and V2; in V2 it is followed by *arrival_span_days*, *amount_over_user_avg* and *component_size*. Neither permutation importance nor SHAP establishes causal influence on fraud: both describe how the fitted model uses its inputs on this corpus, and correlated features share or trade attribution between them.

## 10.7 System Validation Results

The serving and graph components are validated by software tests rather than by the predictive experiment. On the evaluated code base, the five test modules covering the graph cache, graph semantics, evidence availability, the evidence model and the V2 dataset contain 91 test cases, all of which passed in the most recent run.

The graph-cache tests confirm the following behaviours of the running implementation:

- the incremental maintenance behaves as specified — a new edge creates the payee record, a repeated edge increments it, distinct-payer and repeat-payer counts and the arrival window update correctly, the per-payee aggregate agrees with the edge table, components merge when a payer bridges two payees, a failed update rolls back every structure, and concurrent observations do not corrupt the aggregates;
- a pre-payment check leaves the reputation, payer, graph-cache and component tables unchanged, and it never invokes the full graph rebuild used by the analysis dashboard;
- the number of database queries issued by a check increased by at most two when 400 further payments to other addresses were added to the store;
- a later observation does not rewrite a reading taken earlier;
- the graph family reports itself unavailable for an unobserved payee, and a positional fact is emitted only when payers are actually shared under the stated gates; shape findings do not score twice.

The evidence tests confirm that unavailable families carry no score or facts, that a measured zero is distinguished from an unavailable value, and that no payee feature is ever filled with a population constant. The dataset tests confirm that the V1 columns are unchanged when the V2 channels are appended.

These are functional tests of correctness and boundedness under the tested conditions. They are not performance measurements, and no latency figures are reported, because no latency benchmark forms part of the evaluation artefacts.

# 11. Discussion

## 11.1 What the V1 Results Show

The V1 results separate cleanly by scenario. Account takeover and card testing are detected at high rates, whereas social engineering is detected in about one case in five and mule collection in slightly under two in three. This pattern follows from the information V1 contains. Seven of its twelve features describe the payer's own behaviour, and account takeover and card testing are, in the simulator as in practice, departures from that behaviour: unusual amounts, unusual hours and bursts of activity. In a social-engineering payment the legitimate payer acts at an ordinary time and at an ordinary pace, and the payee is typically new to the payer. V1's only view of the payee is its age, payer count and repeat ratio — which are unavailable for about a quarter of payments — together with whether this payer has paid it before, which is true of many honest payments as well.

The ablation adds a qualification. The payer-only model is not blind to social engineering in this simulator (3.85% recall), because a substantial share of simulated social-engineering payments are much larger than the victim's usual amount. Payee information alone does somewhat better (11.54%), and the combination does better than either half (19.23%). The +7.7 pp difference is modest in absolute terms, but it supports the premise of the architecture on this corpus: for payer-authorised fraud, payee-side evidence adds to what payer behaviour provides. It does not show that the V1 feature set is sufficient for this scenario, and at 19.23% recall it plainly is not.

## 11.2 What the V2 Results Show

On identical test rows, the frozen V2 model reaches a PR-AUC of 0.7326 against 0.4860 for V1. Recall at the 1% false-positive budget rises from 49.3% to 78.0%, and social-engineering recall from 19.23% to 65.93%, at a cost of 15 additional false positives. The observed improvement is consistent with the broader feature representation capturing additional simulated contextual signals. However, these signals are generated by the same simulator that generates the labels, so the size of the improvement should not be interpreted as a direct estimate of real-world gain.

Two features of the simulator make this caution more than a formality. First, the scenario-conditional probabilities that govern the message, link and intent channels were chosen by the designer. They were chosen to overlap, but whether real scam payments differ from real legitimate payments in the same direction and to the same degree has not been measured. Second, the message features include the output of a coercion model fitted on the same templated message pool from which V2 draws its messages, so part of the message channel's contribution reflects recognition of familiar templates. The ablation shows that, in this simulator, the message channels are what move social-engineering recall and the graph channels are what move mule-collection recall. This is a statement about how the simulator encodes the two scenarios, and it agrees with the intuition behind the design, but it is not independent confirmation of that intuition.

What the V2 experiment does establish is narrower but still useful. The classical learner, given explicit availability indicators and NaN-coded absences, can use a heterogeneous 47-feature representation in which whole groups are often missing — messages are absent for many payments, graph features whenever payee history is hidden, and intent whenever none is stated — without the absence itself dominating, and with calibrated output. That is a property of the representation and learning setup, and it would carry over to real data in a way that the specific metric values would not.

## 11.3 Graph-Derived Evidence

The incremental payer–payee graph is intended to provide evidence that is invisible from a single payer's history: how many distinct people pay an address, whether they return, how quickly they arrived, and whether they also pay another address that shows the same pattern. Two things have been shown about it, and they should be kept apart. The predictive experiments evaluate the contribution of graph-derived feature channels under a controlled synthetic simulation: in the ablation, adding the graph group raises PR-AUC and mule-collection recall, and removing it lowers both. The incremental graph implementation itself is validated separately through software tests, which show that it maintains its aggregates correctly, is read without being modified or rebuilt, and issues a bounded number of queries per check under the tested conditions.

What has not been shown is the link between the two: that the running graph, populated by real confirmations and imported statements, produces feature values with the distribution the simulator assumes, or that its rule-based positional finding identifies collection accounts at any particular rate. Establishing that would require a time-ordered ledger replayed through the implementation, which is future work. The graph features are also intentionally limited. They are explicit, a finding can be stated in a sentence, and they are computable within one request; but they capture only patterns specified in advance, and a pattern outside that specification will not be seen.

## 11.4 Missing Evidence

The treatment of missing payee history is the design choice most directly supported by the evidence of this study, although the support comes from the construction of the pipeline rather than from a predictive comparison. Substituting a favourable default for a missing history — as an earlier version of the pipeline did — describes every unknown address as moderately established. That is the worst default for detecting new collection accounts, whose defining property is that they have no history. Substituting an unfavourable default would warn on most first payments to strangers, which are overwhelmingly legitimate: 98.3% of payments to unobserved payees in this corpus are legitimate. Representing the absence explicitly, as NaN with an availability indicator, lets the model learn a response to "unknown" from data, and lets the aggregation exclude the family rather than guess.

The corpus shows a higher fraud rate among rows with hidden history (1.729% against 1.005%). This is a property of how the simulator relates visibility to payer count, and the tests exist precisely to keep that dependence small. No experiment here establishes that missingness is predictive of fraud in real data, and the architecture does not treat it as such: an unavailable family contributes no risk.

## 11.5 Risk Versus Confidence

Reporting risk and coverage as separate outputs addresses a failure mode of single-score systems: a low score produced by a lack of evidence looks the same as a low score produced by reassuring evidence. With the two separated, a payment can be reported as low-risk with FULL evidence, low-risk with MINIMAL evidence, or high-risk with limited evidence, and each report means something different. Coverage measures how much evidence was available. It is not the probability that the verdict is correct, and high coverage does not mean that a payment is safe; it means that more of the designed evidence was examined. Whether payers understand and act on this distinction is a question for user studies, which were not conducted.

## 11.6 Operational Interpretation

The four verdicts are advisory states on a risk scale. APPROVE indicates that the aggregated risk is below the configured intervention threshold; it is not a guarantee that the payment is legitimate. WARN indicates evidence that the payer should see before deciding. STEP_UP indicates risk at which the design recommends verifying the payee through an independent channel before proceeding. BLOCK is the highest-risk band, or the result of a hard-block condition such as a malformed address or an amount above the rail limit, and represents a recommendation not to proceed. The system does not hold or stop payments in any band.

The classifier results also bear on the operational burden that any advisory layer imposes. At the 1% false-positive budget, the frozen V2 model raises 572 alerts per 30,000 test payments — an alert rate of 1.9% — and slightly more than half of them (295) concern legitimate payments; for V1, 280 of 455 alerts are false. The balanced class weighting used in training shifts the model's attention toward the rare class, and the isotonic calibration returns the probabilities to the observed scale, but neither changes the underlying trade-off: at a fixed false-positive rate, precision depends on prevalence. If real prevalence were lower than the simulated 1.183%, the same false-positive rate would produce a larger share of false alerts among all alerts, and precision and PR-AUC would fall even with unchanged discrimination. Accuracy, which exceeds 98% for both models only because legitimate payments dominate, conveys none of this and is not used to judge the models. In the full architecture, the classifier's flag is one input: its score is bounded, and the verdict also depends on the other families. How the aggregation changes the alert burden relative to the classifier alone was not measured (Section 12.4). The band boundaries and the classifier's score ceiling are fixed design parameters; this study did not evaluate alternative settings or the distribution of verdicts on labelled data, so no policy recommendation about where the boundaries should lie follows from these results.

## 11.7 Comparison With Prior Work

The comparison with prior work is conceptual; no experiment in this study compares performance with another published system.

Transaction-level fraud models of the kind reviewed in Section 2 assess a completed or authorised transaction chiefly from the account holder's history [2], [3]. The V1 results illustrate their known limitation for payer-authorised fraud: payer behaviour identifies account takeover well but social engineering poorly. The present architecture keeps a payer-behaviour classifier but places it among payee-side, relational and contextual evidence and runs before submission rather than after.

Spade+ [5] addresses dynamic graphs through incremental dense-subgraph detection, maintaining suspicious dense blocks under edge insertions over a large transaction graph. PGLTSE [7] learns temporal node representations with graph neural networks over transaction sequences. Both operate on graph data at a scale and completeness that a single advisory deployment does not observe. The present system uses an incremental payer–payee graph with explicit graph features, a classical calibrated classifier and evidence aggregation; it is not a graph neural network and performs no representation learning. The difference is one of design goals — interpretability of each finding and bounded per-request cost on a small, partially observed graph — and not a claim that explicit features match or exceed learned representations. Whether graph learning would add value in this setting is an open question that these experiments do not address.

UPI-specific work such as Secure UPI [8] and the studies cited in Section 2.5 motivate the problem, but the evidence base for UPI fraud detection remains thin, largely because labelled UPI data are not public — the same constraint that led this study to a simulator.

Finally, the treatment of calibration and explanation follows established literature. Isotonic calibration [17], [18] and the Brier score [19] are standard, and precision–recall evaluation under imbalance follows [11], [12]. Where this study departs from common practice is in how explanations are presented. User-facing reasons come from evidence findings tied to named facts, and SHAP [20] is kept as an offline analysis tool whose computation on the uncalibrated booster is stated explicitly.

# 12. Threats to Validity and Limitations

## 12.1 Synthetic Data

No real UPI transaction data were used. All predictive results come from a simulator whose populations, behaviours, scenarios and overlaps were specified by the authors. The fraud prevalence of 1.183% and the scenario mixture (50% social engineering, 22% mule collection, 20% account takeover, 8% card testing) are experimental settings, not estimates of the prevalence or composition of UPI fraud. Metrics that depend on prevalence, notably precision and PR-AUC, would change at a different base rate even for an unchanged model. Conclusions drawn from the simulator hold only to the extent that its generating processes resemble real fraud and real legitimate behaviour, and that resemblance has not been measured. The simulator also encodes the designer's beliefs about which features distinguish fraud — for example, that simulated social-engineering payments are often much larger than the victim's usual amount, and that collection accounts accumulate one-time payers quickly. Where those beliefs are wrong, the model will have learned a pattern that does not exist, and the evaluation, which draws from the same simulator, cannot reveal it. Two separability artefacts were found and removed from earlier versions of the generator; others may remain undetected.

## 12.2 Graph Evaluation Limitation

The predictive evaluation does not execute the incremental graph cache. Because the corpus consists of independent rows with no payee identities or chronology, V2 graph features are sampled by the simulator from scenario-conditional distributions that follow the production gates. The graph implementation is validated through software tests. The graph-channel results in Table 7 therefore describe the value of graph-derived features whose distribution the simulator assumes; they are not an end-to-end evaluation of the graph implementation, and the rule-based graph family's positional finding was not evaluated on labelled data at all.

## 12.3 Message Template Overlap

The message-coercion model was trained on the templated pool of 1,022 messages from which the V2 simulator draws message texts. The message channel's evaluation therefore overlaps with the templates used to train one of its features, and the pool itself was written for the project rather than collected from real scam traffic. The message-channel results should not be interpreted as evidence that the system generalises to unseen, real-world scam language, which varies in wording, language, script and structure far more than a templated pool. External validation on independent real-world message corpora is required before any such claim can be made.

## 12.4 Full-System Evaluation Limitation

The complete ten-family evidence aggregation and verdict system was not evaluated as a single end-to-end predictive benchmark. The predictive results evaluate the classifier component, and only one family of the ten. The rule-based families, the damped-maximum aggregation, the fact-aware corroboration bonus and the verdict bands were validated by implementation and integration tests for correctness, not for detection performance. How often the full system issues each verdict for fraudulent and legitimate payments, and whether the aggregation improves on the classifier alone, are therefore not established by this study.

## 12.5 V1 Temporal Feature Limitation

When a payer's usual hour is unavailable, V1 records *hours_from_usual* as 0. This conflates an unknown or unmeasured deviation with a measured zero deviation — a payment made exactly at the payer's usual hour. The V1 artefact is frozen and has not been changed, and V2 inherits the same encoding for this feature. The effect of this conflation on the reported metrics was not isolated.

## 12.6 Timestamp and Replay Limitations

The pre-payment temporal boundary is enforced structurally in the live path: the check is read-only and a payment is recorded only after the payer confirms it. The reputation aggregates and the graph, however, are read in their current state and are not time-indexed. Replaying historical payments against a store that already contains later payments therefore does not reproduce the pre-payment state, and no claim is made that arbitrary replay is equivalent to a live check. In the offline corpus, the temporal boundary holds by construction because each row is sampled as a pre-payment state; the offline experiments consequently do not test the live path's temporal handling. Imported statements add a further dependency on correct timezone normalisation and on the completeness of counterparty identifiers, which vary by bank format.

## 12.7 External Verification

The prototype has no connection to NPCI, any bank or any payment service provider and no external source of payee verification. The VERIFIED entity status can arise only from an external verification record, and no such records exist for the payees in this study. No experimental payee is externally verified, and the system's payee-side evidence is limited to what its own users and imported statements have observed.

## 12.8 Distribution Shift

Real deployments may differ from the simulator in every dimension that matters: payer spending and timing behaviour, the language and channels of scam messages, the size and composition of merchant populations, VPA naming patterns and handle distributions, the structure and density of the payment graph, the proportion of payees with observable history, and fraud prevalence and its composition. Fraud patterns also change over time as attackers adapt to controls [3], and a model fitted once on a fixed simulator has no mechanism to track that drift. Any of these shifts could change both discrimination and calibration substantially. The observation mask in particular assumes a specific relation between a payee's number of payers and the chance that the deployment has seen it; in a real deployment that relation depends on the size and composition of the user base, and a small deployment would see far fewer payees than the 75.5% of rows with visible history in this corpus. The threshold selected on simulated validation data would also need to be re-selected on real data, since a false-positive budget met on the simulator is not guaranteed elsewhere.

## 12.9 Calibration and Model Stability

Calibration was fitted and assessed on data from one simulator. Isotonic regression is flexible and can overfit when positive examples are few; here each of the five calibrators was fitted on a held-out fold of the training split containing roughly 170 fraudulent examples, and the reliability curve's intermediate bins contain between tens and a few hundred test payments. Calibration quality on real data, at a different base rate, cannot be inferred from these results. All results come from one seed and one split, with no confidence intervals or repeated runs, so the stability of the metrics under resampling was not quantified. Differences of a few points between ablation rows in particular may lie within run-to-run variation.

## 12.10 Security and Deployment

Several deployment concerns remain open in the prototype. The graph endpoint returns the stored payer-to-payee edge list to any authenticated caller, which exposes other users' payment relationships. The health endpoint, which requires no authentication, reports the library versions the model was trained with. The assistant endpoint returns provider error text in a diagnostic field of its response. Payee reports are counted by distinct reporter but are not rate-limited, so the reputation signal is exposed to report flooding from multiple accounts. These issues would need to be resolved before any deployment beyond a controlled prototype. More generally, a pre-payment advisory layer is itself an attack surface: an adversary who can observe its verdicts can probe which payees and amounts pass, and one who can submit reports or confirmations can attempt to shape the reputation and graph evidence. The prototype's evidence sources were not evaluated against deliberate manipulation of this kind.

## 12.11 Reproducibility Versus Realism

The simulator makes the study fully reproducible. Every row, split, threshold and metric can be regenerated from a seed and hashed artefacts, the V1 columns are verifiably unchanged between representations, and the test split played no part in any decision. The same property limits ecological validity: the ground truth is exact because it was designed, and the signals are informative because they were placed there. This study therefore provides controlled evidence about the architecture's mechanisms, such as missing-value handling, calibration and the relative contribution of channels under stated assumptions. It provides no evidence about field performance.

# 13. Conclusion and Future Work

This paper has presented an evidence-aware pre-payment risk-assessment architecture for UPI-style payments, designed as an advisory layer that assesses a payment request before submission. The architecture combines payer behaviour with observed payee history, an incrementally maintained payer–payee graph with explicit graph features, and contextual evidence from the payment address, QR payload, amount, message, links and stated purpose. A calibrated gradient-boosting classifier contributes one bounded evidence source among ten. Each family reports its availability and the underlying facts on which it relies. Missing evidence is represented explicitly rather than replaced with a favourable or unfavourable default, and corroboration is credited over distinct facts rather than over modules, which limits double counting. The outputs keep risk and evidence coverage separate, and every contribution to risk is traceable to a named finding.

The empirical observations are from a controlled synthetic evaluation. On 120,000 simulated payments at a fraud prevalence of 1.183%, the V1 classifier used by the running prototype reaches a PR-AUC of 0.4860 and detects 19.23% of social-engineering payments. Combining payer and payee features raises social-engineering recall by 7.7 percentage points over the stronger half alone. The frozen, offline V2 representation reaches a PR-AUC of 0.7326 and a recall of about 0.780 at a false-positive rate of about 0.010, with most of the gain in social-engineering and mule-collection recall. Because the additional V2 channels are generated by the same simulator that assigns labels, and because the message model was trained on the same templates that V2 draws from, these figures characterise the method under the simulator's assumptions. They are not estimates of real-world detection performance. The predictive results evaluate the classifier component. The incremental graph and the evidence-aggregation logic were validated through software tests, and the complete ten-family system was not benchmarked end to end.

The study also documents what the architecture's design choices commit it to. Explicit availability means that a payment with little evidence is reported as such rather than scored as if evidence existed. Fact-level corroboration means that agreement between modules raises risk only when they observe different things. The bound on the classifier's score means that a statistical model alone cannot produce the most severe verdict. And the separation of the live V1 path from the offline V2 research representation means that the prototype's behaviour and the paper's best offline figures are never presented as the same thing. These properties hold by construction and are checked by tests; they do not depend on the simulator, and they are the parts of the work most likely to transfer to a setting with real data.

Several directions follow from these limitations:

1. **Independent real-world transaction data.** Evaluation on labelled payment data from an independent source, obtained under appropriate agreements, is the step that would most change what can be claimed.
2. **Externally validated scam-message corpora.** The message channel should be evaluated on real scam and legitimate messages that are disjoint from any data used to build the coercion model, including multilingual and code-mixed text.
3. **End-to-end evaluation of the incremental graph.** Replaying a time-ordered ledger through the running graph cache would test whether its features and positional findings behave as the simulator assumes.
4. **Temporal replay and chronological validation.** Time-indexed aggregates would allow historical replay that reproduces the pre-payment state, and chronological splits would test robustness to drift.
5. **Unseen scam patterns.** Holding out entire scenarios, or variants of them, from training would test behaviour on fraud the model has not seen.
6. **Independent calibration validation.** Calibration should be re-assessed on independent data and at realistic base rates, with repeated runs and confidence intervals.
7. **Deployment-scale latency testing.** Response time for the check and the observation update should be measured with documented hardware and data volumes.
8. **Privacy-preserving validation with financial institutions.** Collaborative or federated evaluation could allow validation against institutional data without centralising it.
9. **Stronger graph learning, if justified.** Learned graph representations should be considered only if future experiments on real graph data show that explicit features are insufficient.
10. **Integration with mobile and payment-provider systems.** Any integration should be undertaken only under appropriate security review and authorisation, and only after the deployment issues identified in Section 12.10 have been resolved.

None of these extensions is implemented in the present prototype. The contribution of this study is an architecture whose treatment of evidence — availability, provenance of facts, separation of risk from coverage, and advisory verdicts — can be examined and reproduced, together with a controlled evaluation that states precisely what it does and does not show.
