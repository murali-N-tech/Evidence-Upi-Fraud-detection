# I. Introduction

## A. Background and Motivation

India's Unified Payments Interface (UPI) lets a payer address a recipient by a virtual payment address (VPA), a mobile number or a QR code and moves funds between bank accounts within seconds {{rastogi}}. Because such transfers settle almost immediately and are hard to reverse, the practical opportunity to prevent loss lies between the payer's decision to pay and the submission of the payment. Detection after settlement supports investigation, but it cannot recover money that has already been moved onward.

Payment-fraud detection is traditionally framed as classifying completed transactions against the history of the originating account {{bolton}}, under extreme imbalance, delayed labels and drift {{dalpozzolo}}. This framing is weakest for authorised push payment (APP) fraud, in which the legitimate owner is deceived — by an impersonated official, a fake refund or a message that manufactures urgency — and authenticates the payment personally {{braithwaite}}. The payer may then pay at a familiar hour, from a familiar device and for an ordinary amount. The informative observations shift to *who* is being paid, how that payee has been paid by others, what the address or QR payload reveals, and what message prompted the payment.

## B. Research Gap

Relational methods capture such payee-side structure, but they are usually formulated as detectors over a whole graph. FRAUDAR finds dense collusive blocks {{fraudar}}, Spade+ maintains dense-subgraph peeling incrementally on dynamic graphs {{spade}}, PGLTSE learns temporal graph representations for organised fraud {{pgltse}}, and APGNN learns fine-grained behaviour prototypes {{apgnn}}. None of these is designed to supply explicit, per-payment evidence to a payer before submission. UPI-specific work has applied oversampling and gradient-boosted classifiers to transaction features and reported accuracy {{secureupi}}, which is uninformative at a fraud prevalence near 1% {{saito}}, {{davis}}.

Combining heterogeneous evidence before payment raises problems that the reviewed work does not address together: an unreadable source must not silently become a value, "safe" or "suspicious"; several components reading one observation must not count as independent corroboration; and relational evidence must come from the state *before* the payment, since a graph evaluated over a store that already holds later payments leaks the future. Moreover, evaluations typically stop at classifier metrics and do not measure what the complete evidence pipeline does to fraudulent and legitimate payments.

## C. Approach and Contributions

This paper studies an advisory pre-payment risk-assessment layer for UPI-style payments. It is not a bank, payment service provider (PSP), switch or National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) system; it neither executes nor holds payments, and it is not integrated with any of them. For a payment request, ten evidence families report a score, their availability and the typed facts they rely on; an aggregation rule reports risk separately from evidence coverage and credits corroboration only across distinct facts. The contribution lies in the pre-payment formulation, the treatment of evidence and its evaluation, not in a new learning algorithm:

1. An evidence-aware pre-payment architecture that combines payer behaviour, payee history, payer–payee relationship, an incremental payer–payee graph, a calibrated gradient-boosting classifier and address/QR, amount, message, link and stated-purpose context, with explicit availability semantics and fact-aware corroboration.
2. An incremental payer–payee graph that supplies interpretable relational findings, validated by a chronological replay in which every payment is assessed from the pre-payment state and inserted only afterwards.
3. A robustness evaluation of the calibrated classifier over ten independently generated corpora, a feature-family ablation and a message-template-disjoint leakage control, comparing a 12-feature baseline (V1) with a 47-feature research representation (V2).
4. An end-to-end evaluation of the complete ten-family pipeline through its running assessment function, which separates continuous risk from the advisory verdict mix and attributes both to individual evidence families.

All experiments use synthetic data because labelled UPI fraud data are not publicly available; results characterise controlled synthetic conditions, not real-world performance.
