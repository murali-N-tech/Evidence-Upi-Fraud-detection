*Abstract*—Instant payment systems such as India's Unified Payments Interface (UPI) settle within seconds, so the opportunity to prevent payer-authorised deception lies before the payment is submitted, when the deceived payer's own behaviour may look normal. This paper proposes an advisory pre-payment risk-assessment architecture in which ten evidence families — payer behaviour, payee history, relationship, an incremental payer–payee graph, a calibrated gradient-boosting classifier, and address/QR, amount, message, link and stated-purpose context — declare their availability and the facts they rely on, so that risk is reported separately from evidence coverage and corroboration is credited only across distinct observations. Because labelled UPI fraud data are not public, all evaluation is synthetic. Over ten independently generated corpora of 120,000 payments, a 47-feature research representation (V2) outperforms the 12-feature prototype model (V1) on every seed (PR-AUC 0.720 ± 0.023 vs. 0.485 ± 0.022 at a realised false-positive rate of 0.99%), with gains concentrated in social engineering and mule collection and unchanged under a template-disjoint message control. A chronological replay of [[F:rows_total]] payments through the running assessment path verifies that every assessment uses only pre-payment state and that the incremental graph is temporally correct. The same replay shows that the complete system ranks fraud better than its classifier alone (risk PR-AUC [[F:risk_pr]] vs. [[F:lv_pr]]) but, with fixed rule weights, blocks [[F:legit_block]]% of legitimate payments in a mature store, and that graph features computed from the replayed ledger do not reproduce the gain attributed to simulated graph features. The results characterise controlled synthetic conditions, not real-world performance.
