
# V. Conclusion

This study investigated pre-payment risk assessment for UPI-style payments, where payer-authorised deception can leave the payer's own behaviour unremarkable. It proposed an advisory architecture in which ten evidence families — payer behaviour, payee history, relationship, an incremental payer–payee graph, a calibrated gradient-boosting classifier and address/QR, amount, message, link and purpose context — declare their availability and the facts they rely on, so that risk is reported separately from coverage and corroboration is credited only across distinct observations.

Four experiments on synthetic data support three conclusions. First, the 47-feature V2 representation outperformed the 12-feature V1 baseline on all ten corpora (PR-AUC 0.720 ± 0.023 vs. 0.485 ± 0.022 at the same realised FPR), mainly for social engineering and mule collection, and a template-disjoint control left this unchanged. Second, a chronological replay through the running system verified that every assessment used only pre-payment state and that the incremental graph is temporally correct. Third, the complete pipeline ranks fraud better than its classifier alone, but its fixed rule weights and verdict bands intervene on far more legitimate payments than the classifier's budget, and graph features computed from a replayed ledger did not reproduce the gain attributed to simulated graph features.

These results establish the internal consistency of the evidence model and the temporal soundness of the graph, and show which components drive the system's decisions; they do not establish real-world detection performance. Future work should learn the evidence-to-verdict mapping from labelled replays, address cold-start payee evidence, and evaluate the architecture on real, privacy-protected payment data and multilingual scam messages.
