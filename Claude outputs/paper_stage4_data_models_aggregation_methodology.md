# 6. Data Construction and Feature Engineering

## 6.1 Synthetic Transaction Corpus

Labelled UPI fraud data held by banks, payment service providers and the national switch are not publicly released. This study therefore uses no private NPCI data, no bank-owned transaction corpus and no production dataset from any payment service provider. The model is trained and evaluated on a synthetic corpus built by a purpose-written simulator, in the tradition of agent-based payment simulators such as PaySim [16]. The corpus serves two purposes: it supports controlled experiments in which the generating process is known, and it makes every reported number reproducible from a fixed seed. It does not, and cannot, establish how the system would perform on real UPI traffic.

The corpus is produced by a single generator call with 120,000 transactions, a nominal fraud rate of 0.012, 2,000 simulated payers and seed 42. The realised corpus contains 1,419 fraudulent transactions, a prevalence of 1.183%. Each simulated payer is assigned a typical payment amount drawn from a right-skewed log-normal distribution (median of approximately Rs 270), a usual hour of activity, a daily transaction rate and a number of habitual payees. A minority of payers (12%) belongs to a segment whose large payments — rent, fees, deposits — are ordinary. Without that segment, large amounts would occur almost exclusively in fraudulent rows, and "amount is large" would become an artefact of the simulated population rather than a property of fraud. All amounts are bounded by the per-transaction UPI limit, and both classes can reach it.

Each transaction is drawn for a randomly selected payer. With probability equal to the fraud rate it is generated by one of four fraud scenarios; otherwise it is generated as ordinary activity. The scenario mixture is fixed at social engineering 50%, mule collection 22%, account takeover 20% and card testing 8%. These proportions were chosen so that payer-authorised deception forms the largest class, reflecting its prominence in public discussion of UPI fraud; they are a design choice for the experiment, not an estimate of the composition of fraud in the UPI ecosystem.

The four scenarios are distinct behavioural processes, and labels are assigned by the process that generated each row rather than by a rule over its features. This matters because a classifier fitted to rule-labelled data can only recover the rule. The scenarios are also constructed to overlap with legitimate behaviour: legitimate payers sometimes send large amounts to payees they have never paid, some account-takeover payments go to payees the victim had already paid, and some social-engineering payees are aged accounts that resemble small merchants. Earlier versions of the generator contained two separability artefacts, which were identified and removed before the reported experiments. The simulator nevertheless remains a simplification; in particular, the degree of overlap between classes is set by the designer and not measured from real data.

An important property of the corpus is that it is row-level. Each row directly represents the state of the evidence at the moment one payment is about to be made — the payer's baseline, the payee's observed history and, in V2, the graph position and message context — sampled from scenario-conditional distributions. The corpus is not a time-ordered ledger replayed through the running system, so the offline features are not computed by the same code that computes them in the live path, with the exceptions noted in Section 6.4. The consequences for temporal integrity are discussed in Section 6.5.

## 6.2 Temporal and Behavioural Feature Construction

The V1 representation contains twelve features (Table 3, Table 4). Seven describe the payer and the proposed payment, and five describe the payee as seen from this payer and this deployment.

**Payer and payment features.** *amount* is the proposed payment amount. *amount_over_user_avg* is the amount divided by the payer's baseline $b_u$, the median of the payer's previous outgoing payments (Section 5.4). *is_night* indicates whether the payment falls between 22:00 and 06:00 Indian Standard Time. *hours_from_usual* is the circular distance, in hours, between the hour of payment and the payer's most active hour. *seconds_since_last_txn* is the time since the payer's previous payment, capped at one day. *txns_last_hour* counts the payer's payments in the preceding hour, and *txns_today_over_avg* is the day's payment count divided by the payer's average daily count.

**Payee features.** *payee_is_new* indicates that this payer has not previously paid this payee; it is payer-relative and can therefore be answered even when nothing is known about the payee. *payee_age_days* is the number of days since the payee was first observed, *payee_distinct_payers* is the number of distinct payers observed paying it, and *payee_repeat_ratio* is the share of those payers who paid more than once. *payee_history_available* indicates whether the payee's history could be read at all.

**Missing payee history.** The live system can observe a payee only if one of its own users, or an imported statement, has recorded a payment to it. For most first-time payments to an unfamiliar address, no such record exists. An earlier version of the pipeline substituted constants in this situation, which amounted to describing every unknown address as a moderately established payee — the most favourable default available, and precisely the wrong one for collection accounts whose defining property is the absence of a track record. The current pipeline does not substitute any value. When the payee's history is unavailable, *payee_age_days*, *payee_distinct_payers* and *payee_repeat_ratio* are set to missing (NaN), and *payee_history_available* is set to zero. When a history exists but the payee has fewer than five distinct payers, the counts are retained, since they are exact, and only the repeat ratio is set to missing, because a ratio over two or three payers does not estimate anything about the address.

The simulator reproduces this condition with an observation mask. The probability that a payee's history is visible rises smoothly with its number of distinct payers, following a saturating curve with half-saturation at nine payers, so that busy addresses are usually observed and new ones usually are not. In the evaluated corpus the mask hides payee history on 29,376 of 120,000 rows (24.48%). Because unobserved payees are, by construction, somewhat more likely to be collection accounts, a small positive dependence between missingness and the label is expected; if that dependence were large, the model could learn "unknown payee implies fraud" and warn on every ordinary first payment. The training pipeline therefore reports the availability bias $P(\text{fraud} \mid \text{hidden}) - P(\text{fraud} \mid \text{visible})$ on every run. In the evaluated corpus the fraud rate is 1.729% among rows with hidden history and 1.005% among rows with visible history, a bias of 0.72 percentage points. Automated tests bound this bias and verify that the availability flag alone does not discriminate strongly between classes.

The distinction between a missing value and an observed zero is preserved throughout. A payee observed with zero repeat payers has *payee_repeat_ratio* equal to 0, whereas a payee whose history cannot be read has the value missing. The two cases would be the same number without the availability indicator and the NaN convention. The histogram-based gradient-boosting learner handles missing values natively by learning, at each split, which branch missing values should follow [13], [14], so "unavailable" becomes a condition the model learns a response to rather than a number it misreads.

## 6.3 Graph-Derived Features

The live system maintains an incremental payer–payee transaction graph $\mathcal{G}_t = (V_t, E_t)$, where $V_t$ contains payer and payee nodes and $E_t$ contains a directed payment edge $(u, p)$ for each pair with at least one recorded payment, annotated with a payment count and first and last timestamps. The graph is updated by the observation path each time a payment is recorded, and an assessment at time $t$ reads its state $\mathcal{G}_{<t}$ (Sections 4.4 and 5.6). The component is a set of explicit graph analytics. It is not a graph neural network, it learns no node or edge embeddings, and it performs no graph representation learning; the quantities it produces are counts, ratios and time spans that are passed to a classical classifier or evaluated by rules.

For each payee the graph maintains the number of distinct payers, the number of repeat payers, the total number of payments, and the first and last arrival and activity times, and it records component membership in a union–find structure. From these, the V2 representation derives seven graph features: *arrival_span_days* (the interval over which the payee's payers first appeared), *lifespan_days* (the interval between first and last activity), *payments_per_day*, *component_size*, *shared_payer_peak* (the largest number of payers the payee shares with any single other payee), *overlap_ratio* (that shared count divided by the smaller of the two payer sets) and *graph_available*.

Raw degree — the number of payers — is deliberately not treated as fraud evidence on its own, since a popular merchant has many payers for benign reasons. The rule-based graph family raises a positional finding only from overlap, and two safeguards restrict when overlap is read. First, overlap between two addresses is computed only when both the payee and its peer have at least ten distinct payers ($\texttt{MIN\_FAN\_IN} = 10$); below that, a high ratio over a handful of payers — for example, a household paying two new local shops — says nothing about either address, and the reading is refused rather than set to zero. Second, overlap with a peer whose own payers commonly return is discounted, because shared customers are the ordinary situation for merchants in the same locality, whereas collection accounts share one-time payers. Component size is recorded as a feature but is not used as a finding, since most addresses in a connected payment network fall in one large component.

In the synthetic corpus, the graph features are not computed by running the incremental graph over a simulated ledger. They are sampled per row from scenario-conditional distributions that mirror the production gates: the graph group is missing whenever the payee's history is hidden, and the overlap ratio is missing whenever either side falls below ten payers. The correctness of the incremental maintenance itself — edge updates, payer and repeat counts, component merges, transactional rollback and read-only access from the assessment path — is verified separately by automated tests on the implementation (Section 9.8), not by the classification experiments. Offline results for the graph feature group therefore measure what such features are worth under the simulator's assumptions about graph structure.

## 6.4 V1 and V2 Feature Representations

Two feature representations are reported, and they are kept separate throughout the paper.

**V1** is the twelve-feature representation described in Section 6.2. It is the representation used by the classifier family in the running prototype's pre-payment check. In the live path, its values are computed from the payer's recorded history, the payee's reputation aggregates and the payer's history with the payee.

**V2** is an expanded representation of 47 features in nine groups: payer timing (2), amount (2), velocity (3), payee (5), relationship (4), graph (7), message (18), link (3) and stated intent (3) (Table 3). It is a research representation. It was trained, frozen with hashed artefacts and evaluated offline; it is not served by the running prototype, and the live classifier family does not use it.

V2 is constructed so that V1 is unchanged. The V2 generator first calls the V1 generator with the same arguments and keeps its output exactly — the same rows, order, labels and twelve column values — and draws every additional column from a separate random stream (seed offset 10,000). An automated check asserts that the V1 columns are bit-for-bit identical after the V2 channels are appended. The two representations therefore share one set of transactions, labels and splits, so differences between them can be attributed to the added channels rather than to different data.

The additional channels are as follows. The *relationship* group records how many times the payer had previously paid the payee, how long ago the first such payment was, and an availability indicator; zero prior payments is a measurement, not an absence. The *payee* group adds whether the payee address is a personal phone-number address. The *graph* group is described in Section 6.3. The *message* group is computed from a message that may accompany the payment: ten binary indicators of coercive cues (claimed authority, urgency, fear, a payment request, a credential request, secrecy, remote-control assistance, a request to verify a payment, a lure and a request to move contact off-platform), the output probability and score of a message-coercion model, a flag for weak cues only, four surface statistics of the text, and an availability indicator. The *link* group records whether a URL is present, a suspiciousness score for it, and whether the brand the link claims conflicts with the payee. The *intent* group records whether the payer stated a purpose, whether that purpose is a refund, an investment or a payment the payer was asked to make, and whether the purpose is inconsistent with the payee type.

It must be stated plainly that these channels are simulated. Whether a message accompanies a payment, which kind of message it is, whether it carries a link, and which purpose is stated are all drawn by the simulator from scenario-conditional probabilities chosen by the designer. The probabilities were set to overlap deliberately — for example, a message is present on 55% of legitimate rows and 62% of social-engineering rows, and legitimate rows frequently carry bank-style notices containing words such as "KYC", "urgent" and "OTP" — but the association between these channels and the fraud label is nonetheless a property of the simulator. The message texts are drawn from a templated corpus of 1,022 messages (364 scam, 557 hard negatives and 101 easy negatives) that was written for the project and not collected from real scam traffic. The message and link features are computed by the same extraction functions that the running service uses, so feature definitions do not differ between training and serving; however, the message-coercion model whose probability forms one of the message features was itself fitted on this templated corpus, so V2 message features can be computed on texts that model has seen. Improvements obtained by V2 are therefore evidence about the architecture under the simulator's assumptions, not direct evidence of production performance.

## 6.5 Feature Integrity and Temporal Boundary

The live pre-payment path must use only information available before the payment decision. Section 5.2 defines this boundary as $\mathcal{H}_{<t}$ and $\mathcal{G}_{<t}$. In the running prototype it is enforced structurally: the assessment path is read-only, and a payment is written to the ledger, the reputation aggregates and the graph only after the payer confirms it. The payer's history with a payee is computed with an explicit strict inequality on the payment time, and payment-velocity inputs are filtered on the same condition. Automated tests check that a pre-payment check leaves all stored graph and reputation tables unchanged and that later observations do not alter a reading taken earlier.

Two qualifications apply. First, the reputation aggregates and the graph are read in their current state. For a live request this coincides with the state before $t$, but for a retrospective replay of historical payments against a store that already contains later payments it does not; the prototype does not maintain time-indexed aggregates, and we make no claim that arbitrary replay reproduces the pre-payment state. Second, in the offline corpus the temporal boundary holds by construction rather than by computation: each row is sampled as the state of evidence at the moment of its payment, and no row's features are derived from other rows. This removes one common route to leakage — aggregates accidentally computed over future transactions — but it also means the offline experiments do not test the live path's temporal handling.

One limitation of the V1 feature implementation is reported as it stands. When the payer's usual hour is unavailable, V1 records *hours_from_usual* as 0. This value is identical to that of a payment made exactly at the payer's usual hour, so an unmeasured deviation cannot be distinguished from a measured zero deviation. V1 is a frozen, hashed artefact and has not been altered to correct this; V2 inherits the same encoding for this feature, although it introduces explicit availability indicators for the groups it adds.

# 7. Fraud Risk Modelling

## 7.1 Learning Problem

Let $\phi(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be the feature vector of a payment request (Section 5.7) and $y \in \{0, 1\}$ its label, with $y = 1$ for a fraudulent payment. The learning task is binary classification: estimate $\pi(x) = P(y = 1 \mid \phi(x))$ from labelled examples, and choose a threshold $\theta$ at which a payment is flagged. The fraud scenario of each positive example is retained for evaluation but is not a training target.

At a prevalence of about 1.2%, a classifier that labels every payment as legitimate is correct on almost 99% of payments, so accuracy says little about whether fraud is detected. The quantities that matter are how highly fraudulent payments are ranked relative to legitimate ones, how many frauds are caught at an acceptable volume of false alarms, and whether predicted probabilities can be read as probabilities. The evaluation is therefore organised around precision–recall analysis, recall at a fixed false-positive rate and probability calibration (Section 9.3), consistent with established guidance for imbalanced fraud problems [3], [11], [12].

## 7.2 V1 Model

The V1 classifier is a histogram-based gradient-boosted decision-tree model [13], [21], as implemented by scikit-learn's `HistGradientBoostingClassifier` [14], wrapped in `CalibratedClassifierCV` with isotonic regression and five folds. Gradient boosting builds an additive model of shallow regression trees fitted sequentially to the gradient of the loss; the histogram variant bins continuous inputs before split search, which reduces training cost and, in this implementation, supports missing values natively. The booster is configured with 300 boosting iterations, a learning rate of 0.08, at most 31 leaves per tree, balanced class weights and a fixed random state. The model is a single boosted-tree learner with a calibration wrapper; five-fold calibration fits five booster–calibrator pairs whose calibrated probabilities are averaged, which is the standard mechanism of the calibration wrapper and not a heterogeneous ensemble of different model types.

Calibration matters in this system because the probability is used as a quantity, not only as a ranking. It is mapped onto a bounded evidence score that is combined with other evidence (Section 5.7), it determines which facts are attributed to the model by counterfactual probing, and it is shown alongside findings. A classifier trained with class weighting systematically inflates probabilities for the minority class; isotonic regression fits a monotone, piecewise-constant mapping from raw scores to observed frequencies [17], [18], restoring the probabilistic meaning of the output without changing the ordering of predictions. The V1 evaluation artefacts also report a class-balanced logistic-regression baseline fitted on the same splits with the same threshold rule, which serves as a reference point for the boosted model.

## 7.3 V2 Model

The V2 pipeline applies the same modelling family to the expanded representation; no second algorithm is introduced. The classifier is `HistGradientBoostingClassifier` with the V1 configuration, wrapped in isotonic `CalibratedClassifierCV` with five folds, and trained on the 47 V2 features.

The V2 configuration was fixed through a validation-only development phase. First, four model families were fitted on the full V2 feature set and compared on the validation split: the V1-configured gradient-boosting model, a random forest, extremely randomised trees and logistic regression (the latter three with median imputation, and logistic regression additionally with standardisation, since they do not accept missing values). Candidates were ranked by validation PR-AUC and then by validation recall at 1% false-positive rate. Second, a feature-group ablation over nested and leave-one-group-out configurations was run on the validation split to choose the feature set. The configuration selected — gradient boosting with all nine groups — was then fitted, calibrated, assigned a threshold on the validation split and frozen. The frozen configuration, the dataset fingerprint (including hashes of the V1 and V2 generators, the message corpus and the message-coercion weights) and the model artefact are recorded, and the configuration records that no held-out test rows were used in any decision. Only after freezing was the model evaluated on the test split.

## 7.4 Class Imbalance

The corpus has a fraud prevalence of 1.183%, and each split was stratified so that it has almost exactly this prevalence. The training split contains 851 fraudulent rows out of 72,000, the validation split 213 out of 18,000 and the test split 355 out of 30,000.

Imbalance shapes both learning and evaluation. On the learning side, the only mechanism applied is class weighting: the booster uses balanced class weights, which scale each class's contribution to the loss inversely to its frequency. No resampling is used; in particular, no SMOTE [15], random oversampling, undersampling or generative synthetic minority sampling appears in the evaluated pipeline. Class weighting distorts predicted probabilities, which is one reason the isotonic calibration of Section 7.2 is part of the model rather than an optional addition.

On the evaluation side, imbalance changes which quantities are informative. ROC-AUC can remain high while the number of false alarms per true detection is operationally unacceptable, because the false-positive rate is computed over a very large negative class [11]. PR-AUC reflects directly how precision degrades as recall rises and is sensitive to the base rate. For an advisory system, false positives carry a real cost: a warning on an ordinary payment interrupts the payer and, when frequent, trains payers to dismiss warnings. The operating point is therefore specified as a false-positive budget — recall is reported at the threshold that keeps the false-positive rate at or below 1% on validation — rather than as a threshold that maximises a symmetric criterion. Accuracy is reported in the evaluation artefacts but is not used as a headline result.

## 7.5 Threshold Selection

Probability estimation, threshold selection and final evaluation are separated. The calibrated model is fitted on the training split. Its predicted probabilities on the validation split are used to compute the ROC curve, and the operating threshold $\theta$ is the one that achieves the highest validation recall among thresholds whose validation false-positive rate does not exceed the 1% budget. That threshold is then applied, unchanged, to the held-out test split, where the realised false-positive rate may differ slightly from the budget.

For V1 the selected threshold is 0.1174, stored to four decimal places; an independent re-derivation from the validation split agrees with the stored value to that precision, and the stored value is the one used by the running service. For V2 the selected threshold is 0.1123. In both cases the evaluation artefacts record that neither test labels nor test features were used in selecting the threshold. The thresholds for the uncalibrated ablation and baseline models of Section 9.6 are selected by the same procedure, separately for each configuration.

The threshold $\theta$ is a property of the classifier. It is distinct from the verdict bands of Section 5.11, which apply to the aggregated risk score $R(x)$; $\theta$ enters the aggregation only as the boundary between the lower and upper branches of the classifier family's score mapping.

## 7.6 Calibration

A predicted probability is calibrated if, among payments assigned probability near $\pi$, a fraction near $\pi$ are fraudulent. Calibration matters for risk communication in this system for two reasons: the classifier's evidence score is a monotone function of $\pi$ with fixed breakpoints, so a systematically inflated $\pi$ would inflate risk; and a probability reported to an analyst is useful only if it can be read at face value.

Calibration is assessed in two ways. The first is the Brier score [19], the mean squared difference between predicted probability and outcome, $\text{BS} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j} (\pi_j - y_j)^2$. Because the Brier score is small whenever the base rate is small, it is reported together with the Brier score of a constant predictor that always outputs the base rate, and with the Brier skill score $1 - \text{BS}/\text{BS}_{\text{ref}}$, which is positive when the model improves on that reference. The second is a reliability curve comparing mean predicted probability with observed fraud rate in bins. Two binnings are reported. Quantile bins are requested, but at a 1% base rate most predictions lie near zero, quantile edges coincide and several bins collapse; empty bins are dropped rather than reported. Uniform-width bins are therefore reported as well, with the number of payments in each bin, so that sparsely populated high-probability bins are visible as such.

## 7.7 Explainability

Model-level explanations are computed in two ways. Permutation importance [22] measures, for each feature, the mean decrease in average precision on the test split when that feature's values are randomly permuted, over five repeats, and is computed on the calibrated model. SHAP values [20] are computed with a tree explainer on a random sample of 200 test rows. Because the tree explainer operates on tree models and the served model is a calibration wrapper, it is applied to the uncalibrated booster of the first calibration fold. The SHAP magnitudes therefore explain that booster's raw output on its own scale. They are not decompositions of the calibrated probability, and they should not be read as the amount by which each feature moves the reported probability. Because isotonic calibration is monotone, the ordering of predictions is the same before and after it, but attribution magnitudes do not transfer across the calibration step, and the booster from one fold is an approximation of the five-fold average.

The running prototype also exposes an explanation endpoint for a recorded transaction, which computes model-agnostic SHAP values for the V1 feature vector against a stored background sample, using the calibrated V1 probability as the explained function. This is an analysis view. It is not part of the verdict computation, and the user-facing reasons attached to a verdict come from the evidence findings described in Section 8.7.

Rankings of feature importance are results and are reported with the other results rather than here.

# 8. Evidence Aggregation and Explainability

## 8.1 Evidence Families

The pre-payment check evaluates the ten evidence families introduced in Section 4.3, and no others: address and QR (`address_and_qr`, covering the VPA, its handle, confusable characters and the QR payload), payee history (`payee_history`, the payee's reputation aggregates), payer behaviour (`payer_behaviour`), relationship (`relationship`), amount context (`amount_context`), stated intent (`stated_intent`), message pressure (`message_pressure`, social-engineering indicators in a message the payer supplies), link safety (`link_safety`), the fraud-probability model (`ml_classifier`) and the graph family (`graph`). Address and QR analysis form a single family in the implementation.

The V1/V2 distinction applies to the classifier family, not to the set of families. In the running prototype all ten families are evaluated for every request, and the classifier family uses the V1 model. Message, link, stated-purpose, relationship and graph evidence therefore reach the live risk score through their own rule-based families, while the V1 classifier sees none of them as inputs. V2 differs in that the classifier itself receives these channels as features; because V2 is not served, its behaviour inside the aggregation is not part of the prototype and is not evaluated in this paper.

## 8.2 Evidence Availability

Each family reports whether it could observe anything, and an unavailable family carries no score, findings or facts (Section 5.3). Availability varies predictably: the classifier requires a known amount, a payer profile and at least five prior payments; the payer-behaviour family requires a payer profile; the relationship family requires an authenticated payer; the amount-context family requires a known amount; and the stated-intent and message families require the payer to supply a purpose or a message. The address and link families are always evaluated, and the payee-history family whenever the address can be looked up in the reputation store.

Availability is summarised by the evidence level (Section 5.10): FULL when the payee-history, payer-behaviour and relationship families are all available, PARTIAL when at least the payer-behaviour or payee-history family is available, and MINIMAL otherwise. Unavailable evidence never contributes to risk in either direction. A first payment to an unobserved payee, from a payer with too little history for a baseline, yields a lower evidence level and lower coverage, not a higher risk; and the same situation does not yield a lower risk than it would if the evidence were present.

The system also defines an entity status for the payee — UNKNOWN, OBSERVED, REGISTERED or VERIFIED — resolved in that order of precedence from the absence of any record, recorded payments, a registered user holding the address, and an external verification record. The entity status is descriptive; it is not an input to the risk score. VERIFIED can be assigned only when a verification record from an external party exists. The prototype has no connection to any bank or payment service provider and generates no such records, so no payee in this study is externally verified, and no statement in this paper should be read as claiming provider verification.

## 8.3 Fact-Aware Corroboration

Several families read overlapping inputs. The payer-behaviour rules and the classifier both examine the amount relative to the payer's baseline, the hour relative to the payer's usual hour, the day's payment count relative to the daily average and whether the payee is new to the payer; the classifier and the payee-history family both depend on the payee's age, payer count and repeat ratio; and the graph family's shape findings describe the same inflow pattern that the payee-history family scores. If agreement were counted by modules, one observation reported under three names would be credited as three independent corroborations.

The implementation therefore counts agreement over facts. Each family declares the facts — from a closed vocabulary of fourteen underlying observations — on which its findings rest. Rule-based families declare facts according to which findings fired: for example, a report or block on the payee is tagged PAYEE_REPORTS, other payee-history findings are tagged PAYEE_SHAPE, and the graph family's shape findings are tagged PAYEE_SHAPE so that they collapse onto the payee-history observation, while only a positional overlap finding is tagged GRAPH_POSITION.

For the classifier, declaring every fact it reads would be incorrect, because one probability would then supply up to six corroborating observations. Its facts are attributed counterfactually, one input group at a time (Section 5.7). For each candidate fact, the inputs underlying that fact are neutralised — the payee-history features are set to missing, a state the model was trained on, and the payer-side inputs are replaced by values describing the payer's ordinary behaviour — and the model is re-queried. The fact is attributed to the classifier only if its probability falls by at least 15% of its original value, and by at least 0.01 in absolute terms. These probes serve only to attribute facts and do not change the classifier's score.

The classifier participates in corroboration through these attributed facts. A source comment in the implementation still carries a constant, `ML_CASTS_AGREEMENT_VOTE = False`, from an earlier design in which the classifier was excluded from agreement altogether; that constant no longer governs behaviour. The set of families excluded from agreement is empty, and the classifier's contribution is controlled instead by distinct-fact counting: when the classifier and the payer-behaviour rules both report HOUR_ANOMALY, that fact is counted once, and the classifier adds corroboration only when it contributes a fact that no other participating family has reported.

## 8.4 Risk Versus Confidence

The system reports two quantities that answer different questions. Risk $R(x)$ measures how strongly the available evidence indicates that the payment should not proceed as requested. Confidence, reported as the coverage score $C(x)$ together with the evidence level $L(x)$, measures how much of the evidence the system is designed to use was actually available. Coverage depends only on which families were available, not on their scores, and it is not derived from the classifier's probability.

Because the two are computed independently, they can combine in any way, and each combination means something different. A payment to an address reported by other users, from a payer with no history, can have high risk and limited evidence: the available evidence is strongly adverse even though much is unknown. A routine payment to a long-established payee, from a payer with a rich history, can have low risk with strong evidence. A first payment of unknown amount to an unobserved address from a new payer can have low risk with limited evidence. In that last case low risk means only that nothing adverse was found in the little that could be examined. Low confidence is never interpreted as low fraud risk, and low risk on MINIMAL evidence is presented as such.

## 8.5 Aggregation

Aggregation follows Section 5.8 and Section 5.9 without modification. Base risk is a damped maximum over the scores of active families: the highest score is taken in full and each further score contributes 40% of its value, with the total rounded and capped at 99. Unavailable families are excluded, so they neither dilute nor inflate the base risk. The damping allows several moderate signals to raise risk above any one of them while preventing a large number of weak signals from accumulating into a high score.

Corroboration adds a bonus only when distinct observations agree. A family participates if it is available and scores at least 20; its facts are pooled with those of the other participating families, and the corroboration strength is the smaller of the number of distinct pooled facts and the number of participating families that reported a fact. The bonus is 0 below a strength of 2 and rises through 6, 14, 22 and 28 to a maximum of 32 at a strength of six or more. The final risk is the base risk plus the bonus, capped at 99, so the system never reports a risk of 100.

Several consequences follow directly from these rules. The relationship family is always scored zero and therefore never participates in corroboration, although it contributes to coverage. The graph family participates only when a positional finding has fired. The classifier's score is bounded at 66, below the BLOCK boundary of 70, so the classifier cannot produce BLOCK without corroborating evidence from at least one other family. The weights used for coverage (Section 5.10) play no part in any of these rules.

## 8.6 Verdict Mapping

The verdict is derived from the final risk by fixed bands: APPROVE for 0–21, WARN for 22–44, STEP_UP for 45–69 and BLOCK for 70 and above. Because risk is capped at 99, the BLOCK band covers 70–99 in practice. The bands are defined once in the implementation and every verdict in the system is derived from them. Independently of the bands, a small set of findings produces BLOCK directly: a blocked payee, a malformed address, a payload that is not a payment request, a missing payee, an empty request and an amount above the per-transaction rail limit. These describe requests that cannot be valid payments to a legitimate recipient.

APPROVE means that the computed risk is below the configured intervention threshold. It does not mean that the payment is safe or legitimate, and it is always returned with the coverage score and evidence level. All four verdicts are advisory: STEP_UP recommends that the payer verify the payee through an independent channel before proceeding, and BLOCK recommends not proceeding; neither holds, stops or reverses a payment.

## 8.7 Explainability Output

Every assessment returns, alongside the risk, coverage, evidence level and verdict, a list of findings. Each finding carries a code, a severity (information, warning, high or critical) and a plain-language message, and each family is represented in the response by its most severe finding while its full list is retained. The response also lists the facts that participated in corroboration and, for each, the families that reported it, so that it can state explicitly when several families agreed about one observation rather than several.

The user-facing explanations fall into recognisable categories. Payer-behaviour findings describe what is unusual for this payer — an amount well above their usual payment, an unusual hour, or unusual activity today. Relationship findings state whether the payer has paid this payee before, and whether a match rests only on a partial identifier or a name from an imported statement. Payee-history findings describe what has been observed about the address, such as its age, the number of payers and how few returned, or reports by other users. Graph findings describe payer overlap with another address. Message and link findings name the coercive cues detected in a supplied message or the problem with a link. The model finding reports the calibrated probability relative to the model's alert threshold. Unavailable families are listed with the reason they could not be evaluated, so that the absence of a warning is not mistaken for the absence of a problem.

These explanations describe the evidence on which a verdict rests. They do not establish that a payment is fraudulent, and a finding such as "first payment to this payee" is presented as ordinary in isolation.

# 9. Experimental Methodology

## 9.1 Experimental Setup

All experiments use the synthetic corpus of Section 6.1: 120,000 transactions from 2,000 simulated payers, with 1,419 fraudulent transactions (prevalence 1.183%), generated with seed 42. Each fraudulent row carries its scenario label — social engineering, mule collection, account takeover or card testing — which is used only for scenario-wise evaluation.

The corpus is divided into training, validation and held-out test splits with stratification on the fraud label. A quarter of the rows is first set aside as the test split, and 20% of the remainder is then taken as the validation split, both with random state 42. This yields 72,000 training rows (851 fraudulent), 18,000 validation rows (213 fraudulent) and 30,000 test rows (355 fraudulent). The evaluation artefacts verify that the three index sets are disjoint. The test split contains 182 social-engineering, 80 mule-collection, 73 account-takeover and 20 card-testing payments. V1 and V2 use the identical split, which is recorded in the V2 artefacts, so the same 30,000 payments form the test set for both.

The split is random rather than temporal. Because the corpus is row-level and not a time-ordered ledger (Section 6.1), there is no transaction chronology across rows to split on, and a random stratified split is the appropriate choice. A consequence is that payments by the same simulated payer can appear in more than one split; since the features describe each payer's baseline rather than payer identity, this does not expose labels, but it means the experiments do not measure generalisation to previously unseen payers.

## 9.2 Evaluation Protocol

Each split has a single role. The training split is used to fit the model, including the internal five-fold cross-fitting of the calibration wrapper. The validation split is used for every decision: the choice of model family and feature set for V2, and the operating threshold for both V1 and V2. The held-out test split is used once, after the configuration has been fixed, to produce the reported figures. The test split was not used to tune hyperparameters, select features, choose thresholds or select among models.

For V1, the evaluation loads the trained artefact, re-derives the validation split from the same calls used in training, confirms that the stored threshold matches a re-derivation from the validation split, and evaluates the stored artefact on the test split without refitting. For V2, the configuration was frozen before test evaluation (Section 7.3), and the frozen artefact records that no test rows were used in any decision. The ablation and baseline comparisons reported later are re-fitted on the training split, with thresholds from the validation split, and measured once on the test split; they are measurements of configurations already decided on validation, not further tuning.

## 9.3 Primary Metrics

Let TP, FP, TN and FN denote true positives, false positives, true negatives and false negatives at a threshold, with fraud as the positive class.

- **Precision** $= \text{TP}/(\text{TP} + \text{FP})$: the fraction of flagged payments that are fraudulent.
- **Recall** $= \text{TP}/(\text{TP} + \text{FN})$: the fraction of fraudulent payments that are flagged.
- **F1** is the harmonic mean of precision and recall for the fraud class.
- **False-positive rate (FPR)** $= \text{FP}/(\text{FP} + \text{TN})$: the fraction of legitimate payments that are flagged.
- **Recall at approximately 1% FPR** is the test recall at the threshold selected on validation under a 1% FPR budget (Section 7.5). The realised test FPR is reported alongside it, since it can differ slightly from the budget.
- **PR-AUC** is computed as average precision, the precision averaged over recall levels weighted by the increase in recall at each threshold [14]. A random classifier's PR-AUC equals the prevalence (about 0.012 here), which gives a baseline for interpretation.
- **ROC-AUC** is the area under the ROC curve, equal to the probability that a randomly chosen fraudulent payment receives a higher score than a randomly chosen legitimate one.
- **Brier score** and **Brier skill score** are defined in Section 7.6.

PR-AUC is treated as the primary threshold-independent metric. Under severe imbalance, ROC-AUC is dominated by the very large number of legitimate payments and can remain close to 1 while precision at useful recall levels is low [11], [12]. PR-AUC reflects the precision cost of each additional unit of recall directly and moves sharply when a model's top-ranked alerts are diluted by false positives. ROC-AUC is reported as a secondary measure.

## 9.4 Confusion Matrix

The ground truth in this evaluation is binary — fraudulent or legitimate — and the primary confusion matrix is the 2 × 2 matrix of the classifier's flag at the validation-selected threshold against that label on the test split. The four verdicts APPROVE, WARN, STEP_UP and BLOCK are advisory outputs of the aggregated risk score, not ground-truth classes, and no four-class confusion matrix is constructed from them; there is no labelled "WARN" payment against which a WARN verdict could be scored. The classification experiments evaluate the classifier family, which is one input to the aggregation. They do not measure the end-to-end verdict distribution of the full ten-family system, which would require the rule-based families to be evaluated on the same rows (Section 9.8).

## 9.5 Scenario-Wise Evaluation

Recall is reported separately for each fraud scenario, using the same threshold as the overall evaluation. Scenario-wise recall matters because aggregate recall can conceal a scenario that is barely detected. In this corpus, account takeover and card testing leave strong traces in the payer's own behaviour, whereas social engineering, in which the legitimate payer is persuaded to pay, leaves weaker ones. A model could therefore reach reasonable overall recall while missing most of the payer-authorised fraud that motivates the system. For each scenario the evaluation reports the number of test payments, the number flagged, the recall and the mean and median predicted probability. For legitimate payments the same computation gives the false-positive rate.

Scenario-wise results are conditional on the simulator's definition of each scenario and on its fixed mixture. They show how the model responds to each generating process as specified, and they should not be read as estimates of detection rates for the corresponding fraud types in the UPI ecosystem, whose relative frequency and behaviour the mixture does not claim to represent.

## 9.6 Ablation

**V1 ablation.** The V1 ablation tests whether combining payer-side and payee-side evidence adds detection of social-engineering fraud beyond either kind alone. The twelve V1 features are divided into a payer half (the seven payer and payment features of Section 6.2) and a payee half (the five payee features). Three models with the V1 configuration are trained — payer features only, payee features only, and all twelve — each with its threshold selected on validation under the 1% FPR budget, and their social-engineering recall is measured on the test split. The reported quantity is the lift of the combined model over the better of the two halves. The current value of this lift is +7.7 percentage points. Note that the payer half is not blind to social engineering in this simulator, because a substantial share of simulated social-engineering payments are much larger than the victim's usual amount. The ablation therefore supports a claim about combined lift, not a claim that payer-only models cannot detect this scenario.

**V2 ablation.** The V2 ablation estimates the contribution of each channel. Using the V1-configured gradient-boosting learner without calibration, models are trained on six nested feature sets: (A) payer timing and amount; (B) A plus payee; (C) B plus velocity, which corresponds to the V1 groups; (D) C plus graph; (E) C plus message, link and intent; and (F) the full V2 set. Two leave-one-group-out configurations are added: full V2 without graph, and full V2 without message, link and intent. The twelve V1 features are included as a reference. Each configuration was first evaluated on validation during development; the measurements reported later re-fit each configuration on the training split, take its threshold from validation and measure it once on the test split. The same protocol is used for the V2 baseline comparison across four model families. Because these re-fitted models are uncalibrated, their Brier scores are not comparable to those of the calibrated frozen models, and the full-V2 row of the ablation differs from the frozen V2 model's figures; the ablation is read for relative differences between configurations. Any gain attributed to the message, link or intent groups is a gain on channels generated by the simulator (Section 6.4).

## 9.7 Missing-Evidence Evaluation

Missing payee history is handled within the main experiment rather than as a separate dataset. The observation mask of Section 6.2 hides payee history on 24.48% of rows, and those rows are distributed across all three splits by the stratified random split, so the test evaluation includes payments to unobserved payees in approximately the same proportion. For every generated corpus the pipeline reports the share of hidden rows, the fraud rate among hidden and visible rows, and the difference between them. Automated tests on independently seeded corpora check that the hidden share lies within a stated range, that no payee feature survives on a hidden row, that the counts survive while the repeat ratio is withheld on thinly observed payees, that the availability bias remains small and positive, and that the availability indicator on its own does not separate the classes strongly.

This protocol is designed to confirm that missing evidence is represented as missing and is not usable as a shortcut to the label; it does not treat missingness as evidence of fraud. The evaluation artefacts do not break test performance down by availability, so no separate figures are reported for payments with and without payee history.

## 9.8 Graph Evaluation

The graph component is evaluated at two levels, and its scope is incremental graph analytics producing explicit features.

At the level of implementation, automated tests on the running store verify: that a new edge creates the payee record and a repeated edge increments it rather than duplicating it; that distinct-payer and repeat-payer counts and the arrival window update correctly; that the per-payee aggregate agrees with the edge table; that components merge when a payer bridges two payees; that a failed update rolls back every structure together; that concurrent observations do not corrupt the aggregates; that a pre-payment check changes no stored table and never calls the full graph rebuild used by the analysis dashboard; that the number of database queries issued by a check does not grow as unrelated edges are added to the store; that a later observation does not rewrite an earlier reading; that the graph family reports itself unavailable for an unobserved payee; that a positional fact is emitted only when payers are actually shared under the stated gates; and that shape findings do not score twice.

At the level of detection, the contribution of graph-derived features is measured by the V2 ablation (configurations D and "full V2 without graph"), subject to the caveat of Section 6.3 that graph features in the corpus are simulated rather than computed by the incremental graph. The rule-based graph family is not evaluated on the synthetic corpus, because the corpus contains no payee identities over which overlap could be computed.

Spade+ [5] and PGLTSE [7] were not re-implemented, and no experiment compares against them. They address different problems with different mechanisms — incremental dense-subgraph detection over a large transaction graph, and temporal graph neural representation learning, respectively — and they require graph-structured data that the row-level corpus does not provide. Their relationship to this work is discussed conceptually in Section 2 and in the discussion.

## 9.9 Latency and Systems Evaluation

The evaluation artefacts for this study contain no latency benchmark for the pre-payment check or for the observation update, and none is reported. The system-level properties that are established are structural and are verified by the tests of Section 9.8: the assessment path performs no writes and no graph rebuild, and its number of database queries remained within two of its original count when 400 further payments to other addresses were added to the store. These properties indicate that per-request cost does not scale with the size of the store under the tested conditions. They are not measurements of response time. Any latency figures should be reported with the hardware, database size and measurement procedure used, and a local measurement on a development machine should not be taken as evidence of performance at production scale.

## 9.10 Reproducibility

The experiments are designed to be reproducible from the repository. The corpus is generated deterministically from seed 42, with the V2 channels drawn from a separate stream seeded at an offset of 10,000, and the dataset fingerprint records the SHA-256 hashes of the V1 and V2 datasets, the message corpus and the message-coercion weights. A cache of the generated V2 corpus is keyed on these inputs and is invalidated automatically when any of them changes. The splits, the learner configuration, the calibration method, the 1% FPR budget and the threshold rule are fixed in code and recorded in the evaluation artefacts, which also record the library versions and the exact commands used.

V1 and V2 are kept separate at every step. The running prototype serves the V1 artefact, and the V1 figures describe that artefact as it stands. V2 was frozen before it was evaluated — its configuration, feature list, threshold and dataset fingerprint are recorded in a frozen configuration file — and it is evaluated offline only. Neither representation's results are pooled with the other's, and V2 results are not presented as properties of the running system.
